The Gig Economy & Worker Classification

What Is the Gig Economy?

The gig economy refers to a labour market characterised by short-term contracts, freelance work, and on-demand services rather than permanent employment. Workers are engaged task-by-task â€” a "gig" at a time â€” often through digital platforms that connect them with customers in real time.

Rather than a traditional employer-employee relationship, gig workers are typically treated as independent contractors who supply their own tools, set their own hours, and bear their own business risks. This model has grown rapidly with the rise of smartphones and platform technology.

Who Are Gig Workers?

Gig workers span a wide range of industries and roles. Common examples include:

  • Uber, Bolt, and Lyft drivers â€” providing on-demand passenger transport via apps
  • Deliveroo, Uber Eats, and DoorDash riders â€” delivering food and parcels on flexible schedules
  • Freelance digital workers â€” designers, writers, and developers on platforms like Upwork or Fiverr
  • TaskRabbit and Handy workers â€” providing domestic and trade services on demand
  • Courier and logistics drivers â€” last-mile delivery workers for Amazon Flex and similar services

How Platforms Control Workers

Despite classifying workers as independent contractors, digital platforms exercise significant control over how work is performed â€” a key factor in employment-status disputes.

Fare & Rate Setting

Platforms unilaterally set fares, delivery fees, and surge pricing. Workers cannot negotiate their own rates with customers.

Route Allocation

Algorithms assign jobs and suggest or mandate routes. Workers have little discretion over which jobs they accept without penalty.

Rating & Performance Monitoring

LANDMARK UK SUPREME COURT RULING

Uber BV v Aslam [2021] UKSC 5

Background

Yaseen Aslam and James Farrar, both Uber drivers, brought a claim before the UK Employment Tribunal in 2016. They argued that despite Uber classifying them as self-employed independent contractors, the reality of their working arrangements meant they should be recognised as workers under the Employment Rights Act 1996 and the Working Time Regulations 1998.

Uber's position was that it was merely a technology platform connecting passengers with independent driver-partners, and that drivers were running their own businesses using Uber's app as a tool.

The Legal Challenge

The drivers challenged their classification on the basis that Uber exercised substantial control over their work: setting fares, requiring acceptance of Uber's terms with passengers, monitoring performance through ratings, and being able to deactivate drivers. They argued this level of control was inconsistent with genuine self-employment.

The Supreme Court Ruling

On 19 February 2021, the UK Supreme Court unanimously upheld the Employment Tribunal's original finding. The Court ruled that Uber drivers were workers â€” not independent contractors â€” under UK employment legislation.

The Court applied a purposive approach, looking at the reality of the relationship rather than the contractual label. Key findings included:

  • Uber set the fare â€” drivers could not negotiate
  • Uber imposed contractual terms on drivers
  • Uber controlled driver acceptance rates and could deactivate accounts
  • Uber restricted communication between driver and passenger

Significance

The ruling meant Uber drivers became entitled to National Minimum Wage (calculated from when they log into the app, not just when carrying passengers), paid annual leave of 5.6 weeks per year, and rest break entitlements. It set a major precedent for gig-economy platforms across the UK and influenced debates globally.

EMPLOYMENT CHALLENGES ACROSS THE CONTINENT

The African Context: Informal & Casual Work

Millions of workers across Africa â€” in agriculture, construction, hospitality, and transport â€” face employment challenges that mirror and predate the gig-economy debate.

Agriculture

Seasonal farm workers across sub-Saharan Africa are hired informally for planting and harvest seasons. Most lack written contracts, receive cash wages below minimum standards, and have no access to social security or paid leave. In Rwanda, smallholder tea and coffee pickers on estates often work under verbal arrangements with no formal employment documentation.

Construction

Construction sites across African cities rely heavily on casual daily-paid labourers. Workers are hired by the day or week, with no guarantee of continued work. Injuries are common, yet workers rarely have access to occupational health coverage or compensation schemes. Job insecurity means workers cannot challenge unsafe conditions without risking dismissal.

Hospitality

Hotels, restaurants, and catering businesses across Africa â€” including Rwanda's growing tourism sector â€” employ large numbers of casual workers. These workers may be called in only when needed, receive no sick pay, and are excluded from pension schemes. The O'Kelly problem of establishing a continuous employment relationship is directly relevant here.

Transport & Delivery

Motorcycle taxi operators (known as boda boda in East Africa or moto in Rwanda) and app-based delivery riders face classification challenges similar to Uber drivers. Platforms operating in Kigali and Nairobi classify riders as independent operators, yet control their routes, fares, and access to work through apps â€” raising the same employment-status questions as Uber BV v Aslam.

Common Employment Challenges in Africa

Lack of Written Contracts

Many workers across Africa are engaged verbally. Without written contracts, workers cannot prove the terms of their employment, making it difficult to enforce any rights they may have. This is particularly prevalent in agriculture, domestic work, and informal trade.

Unstable Employment & Irregular Income

Casual and seasonal work means income fluctuates dramatically. Workers cannot plan financially, access credit, or meet basic needs consistently. This vulnerability makes workers reluctant to assert rights for fear of losing even irregular work.

Limited Social Protection

Formal social security systems in many African countries cover only workers in the formal sector. Informal and casual workers are excluded from pension schemes, unemployment insurance, and health coverage â€” leaving them entirely exposed to economic shocks.

Minimum Wage Challenges

Even where minimum wage laws exist, enforcement in the informal sector is weak. Employers in agriculture and domestic work routinely pay below legal minimums. Workers lack the knowledge or resources to pursue claims, and labour inspectorates are often under-resourced.

Unfair Dismissal & Job Insecurity

Casual workers can be dismissed without notice or reason. Because they lack continuous employment, they often fall outside unfair dismissal protections even where such laws exist. The power imbalance between employer and worker is acute in labour-surplus economies.

Difficulties Enforcing Rights

Labour courts and tribunals exist in most African countries, but accessing them requires resources, legal knowledge, and time that most informal workers do not have. Fear of retaliation, lack of trade union representation, and weak institutional capacity further limit enforcement.

Rwanda: Specific Legal Context

Rwanda has made significant strides in formalising its labour framework. The Labour Law (Law No. 66/2018 of 30/08/2018) governs employment relationships and provides protections including written contract requirements for certain categories of workers, minimum notice periods, and provisions against unfair dismissal.

However, important distinctions apply:

  • Rwanda's labour law applies primarily to formal employment relationships. Workers in the informal sector â€” the majority of Rwanda's workforce â€” are largely outside its scope.
  • The Rwanda Social Security Board (RSSB) provides pension and health coverage, but enrolment is linked to formal employment. Casual and gig workers are typically not enrolled.
  • Rwanda does not have a statutory national minimum wage in the same form as the UK. Wage floors vary by sector and are set through collective agreements or ministerial orders where they exist.
  • UK employment law does not apply in Rwanda. Protections established in cases like Uber BV v Aslam are specific to UK jurisdiction and cannot be directly relied upon by Rwandan workers.

The growth of app-based transport platforms in Kigali raises questions about whether Rwanda's existing labour framework adequately addresses the employment status of platform workers â€” a debate that mirrors the global gig-economy conversation but must be resolved within Rwanda's own legal and regulatory context.

HISTORICAL EMPLOYMENT LAW

The O'Kelly Problem

A 1983 Court of Appeal case that defined the casual worker dilemma â€” and whose core questions remain unresolved in the gig economy today.

COURT OF APPEAL, 1983

O'Kelly v Trusthouse Forte plc [1983] QB 90

Mr O'Kelly and other casual banqueting waiters worked regularly for Trusthouse Forte at the Grosvenor House Hotel in London. They were known as "regulars" â€” given preference for work over other casual staff â€” but had no guaranteed hours and were engaged only when the hotel needed them for specific events.

When the workers were dismissed after attempting to join a trade union, they brought unfair dismissal claims. The central question was whether they were employees under a contract of employment â€” a prerequisite for unfair dismissal protection at the time.

The Court of Appeal held that the workers were not employees. Despite their regular attendance and the mutual expectation of work being offered and accepted, there was no mutuality of obligation â€” the hotel was not obliged to offer work, and the workers were not obliged to accept it. Without this ongoing obligation, no continuous contract of employment could be established.

Mutuality of Obligation

The O'Kelly case established that for a contract of employment to exist, there must be a mutual obligation: the employer must be obliged to offer work, and the worker must be obliged to accept it. Casual arrangements where either party can walk away fail this test.

The Continuity Problem

Employment rights such as unfair dismissal protection require a period of continuous employment. Casual workers who work intermittently struggle to establish continuity, even if they have worked for the same employer for years.

Reality vs. Label

Courts look beyond contractual labels to the reality of the working relationship. However, in O'Kelly, even the reality of regular work was insufficient without the legal foundation of mutual obligation.

Relevance to Modern Gig Work

Gig platforms argue that because workers can log off at any time and the platform is not obliged to provide work, there is no mutuality of obligation â€” echoing the O'Kelly defence. The Uber v Aslam ruling challenged this by finding that the relevant period was when drivers were logged into the app and available, not the entire relationship.

Why Employment Status Matters

The O'Kelly problem illustrates why employment classification is not merely a technical legal question â€” it determines whether a worker has any meaningful legal protection at all. A worker classified as self-employed or a casual with no employment contract may have:

No unfair dismissal protection
No statutory redundancy pay
No right to written statement of terms
No maternity or paternity leave rights
Minimum wage (if classified as worker)
Paid holiday (if classified as worker)

THE FIGHT CONTINUES

From Hotel Casuals to Uber Drivers: The Fight Continues

The story of employment rights is not a story of steady progress toward security and fairness. It is a story of recurring struggle â€” workers seeking recognition, dignity, and basic protections, while those who engage their labour find new ways to avoid the obligations that employment status brings.

In 1983, the casual banqueting waiters at Grosvenor House Hotel lost their case. The Court of Appeal found that without mutuality of obligation, there was no employment relationship â€” and therefore no protection against the dismissal they suffered for trying to organise. The O'Kelly problem was not solved; it was deferred.

Forty years later, Uber drivers in the UK faced the same fundamental question: are we workers, or are we simply independent businesses using a platform? The UK Supreme Court's unanimous answer in 2021 â€” that they were workers â€” was a landmark moment. But it was not the end of the story. Platforms continue to adapt their models, and millions of gig workers worldwide remain outside the protections that the ruling established.

In Africa, the challenge is even more acute. The majority of workers on the continent operate in the informal economy, without contracts, without social protection, and without effective access to justice. Whether they are seasonal farm workers in Rwanda, construction labourers in Lagos, or motorcycle taxi riders in Kigali, they face the same core problems: economic dependence without legal recognition, work without security, and contribution without protection.

The connection between the hotel casual of 1983 and the Uber driver of 2021 is not merely historical. It is structural. As long as labour markets allow those who benefit from workers' efforts to avoid the responsibilities of employment, workers will continue to fight for the recognition they deserve â€” in courts, in legislatures, and on the streets.
